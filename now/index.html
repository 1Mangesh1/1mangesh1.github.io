What I’m up to right now

Three months since the last update. Not because nothing happened — because a lot did, and none of it was at a good stopping point to write about. May, June, July went into work and into one topic I did not expect to swallow a quarter: authorization.

SpiceDB and ReBAC

I went in thinking permissions were a solved problem. Add a role, check the role, move on. That holds until someone asks a question like “can this user see this specific record, because of who they’re related to, three hops away” — and then role checks fall apart, and you end up writing the same subtle query in fifteen places.

SpiceDB is built on the Zanzibar model, which is Google’s answer to that: store relationships, not roles, and ask the permission system a question instead of assembling the answer yourself in application code. Relationship-based access control. document:readme#viewer@user:mangesh, and the interesting part is what it can infer from there.

What I’ve actually been doing is writing schemas, realising they’re wrong, and rewriting them. Getting the model right is most of the work. The query part is easy once the relationships are honest.

The part that took the longest to click is consistency. Distributed permission checks have a real tension between “fast” and “correct immediately after a write,” and Zanzibar’s answer to that — zookies, snapshot reads, choosing a consistency level per call — is a whole subject of its own. I can reason about it and build against it on my own now, which is the bar I was aiming for this quarter.

Keycloak

The other half. Keycloak handles who you are; SpiceDB handles what you can touch. Keeping those separate is the point, and it took me a while to stop trying to make one of them do the other’s job.

Realms, clients, scopes, token exchange, mapping claims to something an application can use without leaking the identity provider’s shape into everything downstream. A lot of it is configuration rather than code, which I found harder, not easier — there are fewer places to put a comment explaining why.

Systems and scaling

Underneath both of those, the thing I actually wanted this quarter: depth instead of breadth. Not another framework. Understanding where reads go, what a cache invalidation costs, which failure happens first when traffic goes up, and why the answer is so often “the database, and later than you think.”

I’ve been reading more than I’ve been shipping. That is a deliberate trade and I expect it to look slow from the outside.

Honest status

No polished project came out of the last three months. What came out is that I now reach for a different design when I see a permissions requirement, and I ask worst-case questions earlier than I used to.

Still carrying the rule from April: ship the bad version. There were fewer of them this quarter because the reading had to come first. That part is done, so the building starts again.

What I’m up to right now

May is April, continued. Still deep in voice agents — but now with better tools. April was raw APIs and duct tape. May is frameworks that handle the boring parts so I can focus on the parts that actually matter.

LiveKit

Found LiveKit and it changed the shape of the problem. WebRTC infra, turn detection, barge-in, transport — all the plumbing I was hand-rolling in April is just there. The Agents framework gives you a sane way to wire STT → LLM → TTS without reinventing the pipeline. Going to spend May porting my broken April experiments onto it and seeing which ones actually work when the infra stops fighting me.

Tavus

Tavus is the other rabbit hole. AI video avatars that can hold a conversation in real time. Different problem space than voice-only — now you have lip sync, expression, and a face that has to feel non-creepy. Mostly experimenting to see what the latency and quality ceiling looks like. Not sure where it lands yet, but the demos are uncanny in a good way.

Webhooks and alerts

The other thread this month: webhooks. Building a small alerting system and learning the hard way that webhooks are easy to send and hard to do well. Signed payloads, retries with backoff, idempotency keys, dead-letter queues, replay tooling. The “send a POST request” version takes ten minutes. The “won’t fall over in production” version takes weeks.

A few things I’m chewing on:

  • HMAC signatures + timestamp tolerance to prevent replay
  • Exponential backoff with jitter, capped retries, then DLQ
  • Idempotency on the receiver side — webhooks WILL be delivered twice
  • Observability: every event needs a trace, every failure needs a reason

Experiments still failing

Carrying forward the April rule: ship the bad version. Several voice + avatar experiments are limping along. A few work well enough to demo. Most don’t. Still learning more from the broken ones.

What I’m learning

Frameworks like LiveKit are a forcing function — they push you toward the right architecture by making the wrong one annoying. Same with webhook discipline: once you’ve debugged one missed delivery in production, you stop cutting corners.

Voice + video + reliable async events is starting to feel like the stack for the next thing I want to build.

What I’m up to right now

April was the month of trying things and watching most of them not work. The theme was experiments — short, scrappy, often broken. I wanted to stop reading about voice agents and actually build one. Turns out the gap between “I understand the API” and “this thing feels good to talk to” is enormous.

Voice AI rabbit hole

Spent a chunk of April deep in voice. Deepgram for streaming STT, Whisper for batch transcription, playing with realtime APIs and TTS providers. The interesting problem isn’t the model — it’s the pipeline. Latency budget gets eaten alive by network, VAD, turn-taking, and the LLM round-trip. A 200ms speech-to-speech target sounds reasonable until you actually measure where the time goes.

A few things that surprised me:

  • Endpointing is harder than transcription. Knowing when the user stopped talking is the actual product.
  • Barge-in support changes the whole architecture. You can’t bolt it on later.
  • Whisper is great for batch, not great for live. Streaming STT is a different sport.
  • TTS quality has gotten weirdly good. Latency is the bottleneck, not naturalness.

Failed experiments

Most of what I built in April did not ship. A voice-driven note-taker that lost context every 30 seconds. An agent that confidently routed to the wrong tool four times in a row. A streaming pipeline that worked locally and fell apart on a flaky connection. Useful failures — each one taught me which assumption was wrong.

Performance tuning

Kept chipping at site performance on the side. Smaller bundles, fewer round trips, killing things that nobody used. Boring work, real wins. The kind of changes that don’t show up in screenshots but make the site feel different.

What I’m learning

Voice changes how you think about latency, state, and error recovery. Text agents can stall for two seconds and nobody notices. Voice agents stall for 400ms and the conversation breaks. Forces a different kind of engineering discipline.

Also: ship the experiment even if it’s bad. The lessons are in the broken version.
